\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Each journal of a railway wheelset carries the axle load into the axlebox through the rollers of the
bearing, the bearing housing, the adapter and, on many bogies, an elastomeric pad. The vertical
stiffness of this load path, which we call the effective support stiffness, falls when the bearing
fits loosen, when the housing, adapter or pad degrades, or when the bearing clearance grows. Such
losses are not detected by the temperature and envelope-spectrum methods used for rolling-contact
faults, which respond to impacts at roller-passing frequencies rather than to a change of stiffness.
The support stiffness does, however, set the frequency of the axlebox resonating on its support, and
on a freight wagon this resonance lies at a few hundred hertz, inside the band in which axle-box
acceleration (ABA) is excited by rail roughness. ABA is already recorded in service for track
monitoring, so the support stiffness could in principle be tracked from data that are collected
anyway.

The obstacle is the excitation. The rail roughness under each pass is unknown, varies along the line
and has a spectrum that falls steeply with wavenumber, so a change of ABA level near the support
resonance can come from the roughness just as well as from the support. Measuring the roughness
directly needs a trolley or a dedicated vehicle, and deriving it from ABA requires the vehicle
transfer function, which is what is to be identified.

The work is motivated by condition monitoring of freight wagons on Indian Railways. The vehicle and
track values that a model of an Indian wagon needs are not yet available to us and are pending data
from the Research Designs and Standards Organisation (RDSO). The method is therefore developed and
assessed here for a representative European freight wagon, a four-axle Eaos/Eanos-type wagon on Y25
bogies running on ballasted UIC60 track, for which the parameters are published. Values that could not
be found are swept over plausible ranges.

\paragraph{Related work.} Axle-box acceleration (ABA) responds to rail roughness filtered by the
vehicle--track system, so any vehicle or track parameter identified from it is confounded with the
roughness. For track stiffness, Carrigan and Talbot~\citep{carrigan2023} fit a model transfer function
to the P2 peak of each section and then derive the roughness spectrum by deconvolution. Shen et
al.~\citep{shen2023} estimate rail-pad and ballast stiffness together with the track-irregularity level
from ABA through a Gaussian-process library of simulations, using frequency bands that include
400--700~Hz; they estimate three speeds separately and identify no vehicle-side parameters. For road
vehicles, Liu et al.~\citep{liu2024} identify suspension parameters and road roughness with an extended
Kalman filter with unknown input, separating the two through the time lag between axles; An et
al.~\citep{an2024} treat the problem in a Bayesian frequency-domain setting; and Guo et
al.~\citep{guo2026} also address road-vehicle parameters with unknown roughness. For railway vehicles, Lebel, Soize and co-authors identify
suspension parameters as a statistical inverse problem~\citep{lebel2019,lebelhal}. Drive-by bridge
monitoring removes road roughness by subtracting responses recorded at different
speeds~\citep{jin2021}, and Bayesian FFT operational modal analysis treats the unknown excitation
spectrum as a nuisance in a frequency-domain likelihood~\citep{au2011}.

To our knowledge, this is the first method to identify the vertical stiffness of the axle-box bearing
support from in-service axle-box acceleration without measuring rail roughness. The support resonance
and the roughness are separated by pooling passes at different speeds and payloads in a single
debiased Whittle likelihood, in which roughness levels are free nuisance parameters. Joint estimation
of vehicle or track parameters with unknown excitation has been studied for track stiffness
\citep{carrigan2023,shen2023} and for road-vehicle suspensions \citep{liu2024,an2024}. There, roughness is handled by deconvolution, a single level parameter, or axle
time lags, and the targets lie below the axle-box support band.

The contributions of this paper are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a multi-pass debiased Whittle likelihood that identifies the effective support stiffness and
damping of a wheelset bearing from ABA spectra of many passes, with the roughness level of each
sub-band a free nuisance parameter, payload entering through a weigh-in-motion measurement, and the
leakage of the finite records handled by the debiased periodogram;
\item a forward model in which the instrumented wheelset is coupled to its neighbours through an
infinite Timoshenko rail, with the phase factors between wheels kept, and evidence that ignoring this
coupling biases the support stiffness by \IgnoreCouplingForty\%;
\item an error budget for a representative freight wagon: the Cram\'er--Rao bound and its dependence
on speed spread and number of passes, Monte Carlo bias and coverage, model-form sensitivity, and
simulation-calibrated intervals;
\item a procedure for detecting changes between monitoring periods that re-estimates the track
foundation and contact stiffness in each period, with detection limits and false-alarm rates under
track drift, and an assessment of which support faults are large enough to be detected.
\end{enumerate}
