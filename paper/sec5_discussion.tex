\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What the method delivers.} The estimator separates the support resonance from the roughness
because the two scale differently with speed: the resonance is fixed in frequency, the roughness
excitation is fixed in wavenumber. Speed spread is therefore the design variable that matters most
(Section~\ref{sec:res-speed}); at a single speed the support stiffness is barely identifiable, and a
spread of 60--110~km/h, typical of a mixed freight route, brings the bound to a fraction of a per cent
with 50 passes. Payload variation and a weigh-in-motion measurement add the load dependence of the
contact and support stiffness. Neither requires a dedicated test: the passes are those a wagon makes
in service, and the roughness never has to be measured. The records must, however, meet the
requirements of Section~\ref{sec:requirements} on sensor bandwidth, calibration, speed, record alignment,
payload and record selection.

\paragraph{Absolute level and relative change.} The absolute level of $k_{B0}$ inherits the error of
every other model parameter, above all the unsprung mass and the foundation stiffness
(Section~\ref{sec:res-modelform}); its budget of a few per cent is an order of magnitude larger than the
statistical error. For monitoring this matters less than it seems, because a model error that is the
same in two periods largely cancels in the change. What does not cancel is a change of the track
between periods, and re-estimating the foundation and contact stiffness in each period removes the
resulting false alarms (Section~\ref{sec:res-drift}). The method is therefore best used as a trend
monitor for each wheelset, with the first period as its own baseline.

\paragraph{Which faults can be seen.} The detection limit of the change is below one per cent for 50
passes per period and roughly halves with 200 (Section~\ref{sec:res-relative}). A loss of housing,
adapter or pad stiffness enters $k_B$ directly and is detectable once it exceeds this limit. Clearance
growth is a weaker signal, because the roller contacts are much stiffer than the rest of the support
and their change is diluted in the series sum (Section~\ref{sec:res-health}). The method is not a
substitute for envelope or temperature monitoring of rolling-contact faults; it covers a different
failure path, the progressive loss of support, which those methods do not see.

\paragraph{Relation to track monitoring.} The same likelihood, with the foundation stiffness as the
target, gives median estimates close to the truth where per-pass P2 fits scatter widely under
corrugation (Section~\ref{sec:res-p2}); its intervals then under-cover, so on the track side it can
screen per-pass estimates but does not yet give calibrated intervals.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
\paragraph{Residual bias and simulation-calibrated intervals.} For the representative wagon (pinned
headline configuration) the frozen estimator has a bias of at most \HLBiasAbsMax\% (Table~\ref{tab:table3_headline}) and a scatter up to
\HLRatioCRBMax{} times the Cram\'er--Rao bound, but the 40~dB Wald intervals still cover the truth
slightly less often than nominal (\HLFortyCov). In the earlier configuration the bias reached
\BDWideBias\% for one seed, and Section~\ref{sec:res-bias} ruled out the obvious causes: the
large-sample (pseudo-true) bias is nil (slope \CalSlope), sensor noise does not change the error, exact
sub-band scoring and a finer table change it by at most \BDMaxDiffExact{} percentage points, robust
(sandwich) standard errors do not restore coverage, and fewer roughness levels do not remove it (no
incidental-parameter effect). Truncation of the debiasing kernel explained about a fifth and is removed by
the wider kernel; the remainder is unexplained and depends on the realisation of roughness, speeds and
payloads. The 40~dB intervals are therefore simulation-calibrated for the stated design (speed range,
payload range, number of passes, roughness model and noise level), and must be re-calibrated for any
other design. The calibration was checked on held-out seeds (Section~\ref{sec:res-headline}); at
20~dB, where no calibration is applied, the held-out coverage differed between the two splits, so the
nominal 90\% should be read as approximate.
For relative changes under random model errors (earlier configuration), the calibrated interval still
under-covers
(\KapCalCovered/\KapCalN): the gain uncertainty $\kappa$ derived from one-at-a-time model errors is too
small, and an interval for the change that holds under realistic model errors remains open.

\paragraph{Load exponent as a clearance indicator.} Clearance growth changes not only the level of the
effective support stiffness but also its dependence on axle load, because a larger clearance shrinks
the load zone more at light load. In principle the load exponent $p$ of~\eqref{eq:powerlaw} could
therefore separate clearance growth from other losses of support stiffness. With the exponent free
alongside $(k_{B0}, c_B)$ and payloads spanning \PayloadMin--\PayloadMax~t, the Cram\'er--Rao bound of
$p$ is \ExpCRBFifty{} for $N = 50$ passes and \ExpCRBTwoHundred{} for $N = 200$, and freeing $p$ raises
the bound of $k_{B0}$ from \KBCRBExpFixed\% to \KBCRBExpFree\% ($N=50$). Under the series reading of
the support (Y25 cylindrical roller bearings, Table~\ref{tab:table1_parameters}), 10 and 20~\textmu m of clearance growth raise the
effective exponent by only \ExpShiftTen{} and \ExpShiftTwenty{}. Detecting a shift between two
periods (one-sided 5\% false alarm, 90\% detection) needs \ExpMDCFifty{} at $N=50$ and
\ExpMDCTwoHundred{} at $N=200$, so neither would be detected, even with 200 passes per period. The exponent is
therefore held fixed in the estimator, and the method reports a loss of effective support stiffness
without attributing it to a mechanism.

\paragraph{Linear contact and model form.} Data and estimator share the linear vehicle--track model, so
the Monte Carlo results do not include the error of the model itself beyond the parameter errors of
Section~\ref{sec:res-modelform}. The wheel--rail contact is linearised about the static load; the
effect of the nonlinear Hertzian contact on the estimates is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:res-inverse-crime}. That check also exposed an unexplained difference of
\GenkB{} percentage points in $k_{B0}$ and about \GencB{} in $c_B$ between two generators that implement
the same linear model; it is small against the detection limit and cancels in changes measured with
one generator, but it shows that the residual bias of $c_B$ depends on details of the simulation that
were not resolved. The model is vertical and two-dimensional, the bogie frame is
held fixed, the wheels are rigid, and the rail pinned--pinned resonance of discretely supported track
is not represented; all of these lie outside or at the edge of the analysis band but have not been
tested against data.

\paragraph{Primary suspension and payload bands.} The primary damping of the Y25 bogie comes from a
friction damper; the values used are low-frequency equivalent viscous coefficients for the empty and the
laden wagon~\citep{dusza2024}, not validated at the 400~Hz of the support resonance, where the damper is
likely to stick and act more as a stiffness than as a damper. The stress test of
Section~\ref{sec:res-primary}, which lets the low-frequency values vary with payload, is therefore likely
to be conservative. It shows that pooling passes over the whole payload range requires a
payload-dependent model of the primary suspension: with a fixed suspension, the error of each period
depends on its payload mix and does not cancel in the change. The method is therefore operated within
payload bands, with the empty band preferred. Co-estimating a payload-dependent primary suspension, which
would restore the full payload range, is left for future work, as is measuring the damper's behaviour at
the support frequency, which the absolute level of $k_{B0}$ also depends on.

\paragraph{Representative parameter values.} The vehicle and track values are those of a
representative freight wagon (Y25 bogies, 22.5~t axle load) on ballasted 60E1 track, taken from
published sources where they could be found (Table~\ref{tab:table1_parameters}). The effective support
stiffness, the support damping, the wheel diametral inertia and the internal geometry of the bearing
were not found for a freight axlebox and are swept over plausible ranges
(Table~\ref{tab:table15_sweep}); the bound and detection limit are reported over each range rather
than at a single value. A specific vehicle requires its own values, above all the unsprung mass.

\paragraph{Roughness and operating model.} Roughness is modelled as a stationary Gaussian process
whose spectrum has the ISO~3095 reference shape times free sub-band levels shared by all passes of a
period, with an independent realisation on each pass that is common to the wheels of that pass
(Section~\ref{sec:obs}); speed is constant within a pass. A roughness spectrum whose shape changes
between passes within a period, beyond what the free levels absorb, is not modelled. Non-stationary features (joints, welds, switches) and speed changes
within a record are not modelled and would have to be excluded or windowed out in practice.
Environmental effects on the support, for example the temperature dependence of elastomeric pads,
would appear as changes of $k_B$ and must be separated by comparing like conditions.

\paragraph{No field data.} All results are from simulation. A field test with an axlebox whose
support stiffness is changed in a controlled way is needed before the detection limits can be
claimed for a real vehicle.
